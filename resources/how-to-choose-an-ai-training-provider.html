How to choose an AI training provider in Australia

By Dylan Bailey, Claude Certified Associate
Updated 13 November 2026 · 9 min read
The short answer

Choose the provider that gets your team using AI in their real work, not the one with the most videos. The best AI training for Australian staff is built for non-technical people, has them practise on their own tasks, teaches skills that carry across tools, tracks whether people actually finish, and keeps supporting them after the course ends. Before you sign, ask any provider one question: what will each person have built by the end?

AI training is now easy to find in Australia. There are one-day workshops, online courses, university short courses, free tutorials from the AI companies themselves and full enablement programs. They can all look similar from the outside, and most of them promise to make your team “AI-ready”.

The difference shows up a month later. Either your people are using AI every day in the work they already do, or the training has quietly faded into a few bookmarked prompts. This guide is the checklist we would use if we were choosing a provider for our own team.

The 9 things to look for

1. It’s built around your team’s real work

Generic demos are easy to sit through and hard to apply. Good training has people practise on their own emails, reports, spreadsheets and recurring jobs, so the first useful result happens during the course, not after it. Ask: what will each person have built by the end?

2. It’s made for non-technical staff

Most of the hours AI can save sit with finance, admin, operations, marketing, sales and customer service, not with developers. Training should assume no coding background, use plain language and give examples for each of those roles.

3. The skills carry across tools

AI tools change every few months. Clear prompting, checking answers, turning a task into a repeatable workflow and handing work to an agent are skills that work in ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot and Gemini alike. Be wary of training that is really a product tour of one app. Our ChatGPT vs Copilot vs Gemini vs Claude comparison covers how the main tools differ.

4. The trainers have real credentials and real builds

Look for trainers who use AI in their own business every day and can show what they have built. Vendor certifications, such as Anthropic’s Claude certifications, are a useful signal that someone knows a tool properly. What matters most is whether they have done the work your team is about to do.

5. Progress is tracked, and managers can see it

Self-directed online courses are easy to start and easy to drift away from. Ask how the provider knows when someone is stuck, what happens next, and what reporting leadership receives. If the answer is “we send a completion certificate”, there is no accountability built in.

6. It covers safe and responsible use

Staff need to know how to check an AI answer before relying on it, what not to paste into a tool, and how your obligations under the Australian Privacy Principles apply. Training should help you set or follow a simple AI policy, not leave it to chance. Our guide to AI hallucinations explains why checking matters.

7. It goes beyond prompting to automation and agents

Writing better prompts is the start. The bigger time savings come from automating recurring tasks and setting up AI agents that work through multi-step jobs. If a program stops at prompting, most of the value is left on the table. See our AI automation examples for small business.

8. Support continues after the course

New features land constantly, and people get stuck on real tasks long after the last lesson. Check how long you keep access to the material, whether it is kept up to date, and whether there is someone to ask once the program ends.

9. The format fits how your team works

A one-day workshop suits a kick-off. Self-paced online training suits teams spread across locations, shifts or time zones. Cohort-based or in-house sessions suit teams that learn best together. The right format is the one your people will actually finish.

How AI training is usually priced

Australian providers usually charge in one of four ways:

  • Per person: a fee for each learner, common for online programs.
  • Per session or day: a set fee for a workshop, whatever the headcount up to a limit.
  • Per team or company: a package price for a whole team or rollout.
  • Subscription or membership: a monthly fee for ongoing support, updates and community, sometimes on top of a program fee.

Rather than compare price tags, compare cost per outcome: what each person will have built, how many hours a week that frees up, and what support is included. Check exactly what the fee covers, including trainer time, how long you keep access, updates and what happens after the program.

12 questions to ask any AI training provider

  1. What will each person have built by the end?
  2. Will they practise on our real tasks, or on generic examples?
  3. Do our staff need any technical background?
  4. Which AI tools do you teach, and do the skills transfer to other tools?
  5. Who are the trainers, and what do they use AI for in their own business?
  6. How do you track progress, and what do managers see?
  7. What happens when someone gets stuck?
  8. How do you cover privacy, data safety and checking AI’s answers?
  9. Does it cover automation and AI agents, or only prompting?
  10. How long do we keep access to the material, and how often is it updated?
  11. What support is there after the program ends?
  12. Can we speak to a business you have already trained?

Red flags

  • Guaranteed savings before they know your work. Nobody can promise hours saved without understanding your tasks.
  • A single-tool product tour. Useful for that tool, but the skills will date quickly.
  • No way to see progress. If managers can’t see who is stuck, nobody will step in.
  • Out-of-date content. Lessons built around old models and features suggest the material isn’t maintained.
  • A certificate as the main outcome. The point is working builds and changed habits, not a PDF.
  • Pressure to sign before a proper conversation. A good provider will want to understand your team first.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best AI training for staff in Australia?
The best AI training is the one your staff actually finish and use in their work. Look for training built for non-technical people, practice on your own tasks, skills that work across AI tools, progress tracking for managers, coverage of automation and AI agents, and support after the course ends. Ad On AI, the AI training division of Gold Coast-based Ad On Group, designed its online program around exactly these points, but the checklist on this page works for comparing any provider.
How long should AI training for staff take?
Long enough to build real habits. A one-day workshop is a useful kick-off, but lasting change usually comes from practice spread over several weeks on real tasks. Ad On AI's program is 24 modules and under 24 hours of learning in total, done at each person's pace, with lifetime access to the material.
Should AI training be online or in person?
It depends on your team. Online, self-paced training suits teams spread across locations, shifts or time zones, and lets people learn on their own tasks. In-person workshops build energy and suit a kick-off. Many businesses combine a short live session with a structured online program.
Do staff need technical skills before AI training?
No. Good AI training for business is built for non-technical staff and assumes no coding background. If someone can write an email and use a spreadsheet, they can learn to use AI well and build simple automations.
Which AI tool should our staff be trained on?
Start with the tool that fits the software you already use, for example Copilot with Microsoft 365 or Gemini with Google Workspace, or Claude or ChatGPT for general work. More important is that the training teaches skills that carry across tools, because the tools change every few months.
How do we know if AI training is working?
Measure outcomes rather than attendance: what each person has built, how many hours a week those builds save, which tasks now run automatically, and how many staff finish. Ask your provider what reporting they give managers and leadership.
